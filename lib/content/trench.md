## How to Use the Trench Calculator

Enter your trench dimensions — **length**, **width**, and **depth** — select your bedding material, and click **Calculate**. The trench calculator instantly returns:

- **Excavation volume** in cubic yards (or cubic metres in metric mode)
- **Bedding material needed** (gravel, sand, or concrete cradle)
- **Backfill volume** — how much soil goes back in after pipe installation
- **Loose spoil volume** — accounting for a 25 % swell factor when soil is loosened
- **Dump truck loads** — based on a standard 14-cubic-yard soil truck
- **Cost breakdown** — excavation labor, bedding material, and off-site spoil hauling

Switch between **Imperial** (ft / cu yd) and **Metric** (m / m³) with one click. All results update instantly.

## Trench Volume Formula

The formula for trench excavation volume is:

> **Volume (cu yd) = Length (ft) × Width (ft) × Depth (ft) ÷ 27**

For metric projects:

> **Volume (m³) = Length (m) × Width (m) × Depth (m)**

**Example:** A 50 ft long × 2 ft wide × 4 ft deep utility trench:
- Volume = 50 × 2 × 4 ÷ 27 = **14.81 cu yd**
- With 25 % swell factor, loose spoil = **18.5 cu yd** (2 truck loads)

## Trench Bedding Materials Explained

Bedding material is placed at the bottom of the trench to provide a stable, level, and properly graded surface for the pipe or conduit. The right choice protects the pipe from point loading and settlement.

### Crushed Gravel / Pea Gravel
The most common choice for gravity sewer, storm drain, and water service lines. It provides excellent drainage and conforms to irregular pipe surfaces. Typical bedding depth: **4–6 inches** (100–150 mm). Cost: **$25–$45 per cu yd** delivered and placed.

### Sand
Used where uniform support is critical, such as delicate plastic pipe or conduit. It compacts easily and is often required by municipal specifications. Cost: **$20–$38 per cu yd**.

### Concrete Cradle
For high-load applications — large-diameter sewer mains under roads, or where ground conditions are poor — a concrete encasement is poured around the pipe. This is the most expensive option. Cost: **$140–$220 per cu yd**.

### No Bedding
Only appropriate for solid-wall conduit in stable, rocky soil that is already at the correct grade. Not recommended for plastic pipe or drainage systems.

## Backfill vs Spoil: What's the Difference?

After the pipe or conduit is laid, the trench must be backfilled in controlled lifts and compacted. Not all excavated soil goes back in:

- **Backfill volume** = Trench volume − bedding volume. This is the amount of native soil (or imported fill) needed to fill the trench back up to grade.
- **Spoil** = The excess or unsuitable soil that must be hauled away. Even if you're reusing native soil as backfill, the **swell factor** (soil expands ~25% when loosened) means you'll have more loose material than can fit back in the trench.

> **Tip:** For projects under 20 cy of spoil, a standard roll-off dumpster is often cheaper than hiring a dump truck. For larger volumes, a 14-cy dump truck at $250–$350 per load is more economical.

## OSHA Safety Requirements for Trenches

Trench excavation is one of the most hazardous construction activities. OSHA 29 CFR 1926 Subpart P sets clear requirements:

- **Trenches 5 feet or deeper** require a protective system: sloping/benching, trench shield (box), or shoring.
- **Trenches 20 feet or deeper** require a design by a registered professional engineer.
- A competent person must inspect the trench daily and after any rain event.
- Water accumulation in a trench is a serious hazard — dewatering is required before workers enter.
- Access/egress must be within 25 feet of all workers.

**Always obtain the required excavation permits** before breaking ground. Most US municipalities require a permit for any trench deeper than 2 feet near underground utilities.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How many cubic yards is a typical residential trench?**
A standard 50-foot-long, 18-inch-wide, 4-foot-deep utility trench is approximately 11 cu yd of excavation. Including swell, you'd haul about 14 cu yd of loose spoil.

**What is the swell factor for trench excavation?**
Standard soil swells approximately 20–30% when excavated (loose). A 25% swell factor is widely used: 10 cu yd of in-situ soil becomes 12.5 cu yd of loose spoil. Clay can swell up to 35–40%.

**How long does it take to dig a trench?**
A mini excavator can dig approximately 50–100 linear feet per hour in average soil at 4 ft depth. A standard excavator can dig 100–300 lf/hr. Hand digging: 5–10 lf/hr per worker.

**Do I need a permit to dig a trench?**
Always call 811 (US) or your local utility mark-out service before digging. Most municipalities require a permit for trenches deeper than 18–24 inches near public right-of-way, utility easements, or structures.

**Can I reuse excavated soil as backfill?**
Yes, if the native soil is not expansive clay, organic, or heavily contaminated. Granular soil (sand, gravel, sandy loam) is ideal for backfill. Clay-heavy soil may require importing granular fill to prevent poor compaction and pipe movement.
